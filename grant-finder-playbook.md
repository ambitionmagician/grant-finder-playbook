# The Grant Finder Playbook — for independent researchers and tiny nonprofits

A grant search should end with a decision, not a long list of links. Sometimes the right decision is to write a proposal. Sometimes it is to ask one eligibility question, offer to join another applicant’s project, or close the notice before spending an afternoon on it. All four outcomes are useful.

This playbook uses U.S. examples. Its running case is a small public-history project, but the methods apply to other fields. Dollar amounts, project names, dates, and sample funder terms are illustrative. For an actual application, use the current opportunity notice, amendments, application instructions, and written answers from the grantmaker. If funded, work from the signed agreement and any documents it incorporates. A listing tells you where to look; it does not settle what you may do. Nothing in this playbook guarantees funding.

## 1. Make a search brief before you look for money

Write down what you would do even if you had never seen a grant notice. Limit yourself to five sentences:

1. What do you need to learn or change?
2. Who has a stake in the result?
3. What work can you finish?
4. What will it cost?
5. Who can take legal responsibility for the award?

If the last sentence is difficult, start there.

Consider Mara, an independent historian working with the fictional Mill Creek Library. A local bus route no longer runs to the old mill. The library holds route maps but has few accounts from people who depended on it. Mara wants to record former riders’ accounts and, with their permission, make an edited collection available through the library.

Her search is not simply for “research funding.” She needs support for her time, participant payments, transcription, accessible presentation, and stewardship of the collection. A travel fellowship that covers only a week in a distant archive will not pay for that work.

Make a brief you can keep beside the search window:

| Search field | Mill Creek example |
|---|---|
| Possible applicants | Mara as an individual; the library only if it agrees to lead and qualifies |
| Place | Mill Creek and the surrounding county |
| Subject and method | Public history, transportation, oral history, community archives |
| Usable award | About $7,000 for the collection; a smaller amount only for a separately viable phase |
| Earliest start | After permissions, staff availability, and funding are confirmed |
| Nonnegotiable condition | Participants can decline public release |
| Project cannot absorb | Mandatory cash match or a long wait for reimbursement without an advance |

Distinguish the **applicant** from the **person doing the work**. An individual fellowship might pay a researcher directly. A project grant might require a tax-exempt organization to apply, employ or contract with the researcher, manage the money, and file reports. A library willing to host a listening event has not thereby agreed to be the applicant. Ask the person authorized to commit it.

If you explore fiscal sponsorship, ask two parties different questions:

- Ask the **grantmaker** whether its rules allow the proposed sponsor arrangement.
- Ask the **prospective sponsor** what oversight, fee, payment schedule, documentation, and sign-off it requires.

Clarify who owns equipment and who is responsible for records after the project. Do not use another organization’s tax identification number as a substitute for an agreement.

## 2. Where the money actually lists

Search by the kind of institution making the award. The page where you discover a name is not necessarily the page where you should apply. For every promising lead, save the grantmaker’s own program page and the current instructions, along with the date you checked them.

| Source | Where to start | What to verify on the official page |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | [Grants.gov](https://www.grants.gov/) opportunity search; agency funding pages | Opportunity number, status, applicant types, amendments, submission system |
| State | State agency sites, grants portals, arts councils, and state libraries | State residency or service area, eligible entity, required registration |
| Foundation | Foundation sites, [Candid](https://candid.org/) tools where accessible, prior grantees’ acknowledgments | Whether proposals are accepted, who may apply, next decision date |
| Corporate | Company community-investment pages and corporate foundation sites | Footprint, exclusions, form of support, open application or nomination |
| Community | Community foundations, local arts funds, library systems, municipal and county programs | Geographic boundaries, named fund restrictions, local cycle and contact |

### Federal

On Grants.gov, search once by subject and again by agency. Filter for open opportunities and inspect the eligibility text, not just the search tags. A forecast is useful for planning but is not an invitation to submit.

Use [SAM.gov Assistance Listings](https://sam.gov/content/assistance-listings) to identify federal programs and their objectives. An Assistance Listing by itself is not a current competition.

If a notice requires an organizational registration or an authorized submitter, verify both early. The person who can draft in a portal may not be the person who can submit. Do not assume instructions for an organization apply to an individual.

### State

Follow the work into the agency that would care about it. A project about the bus route might fit a state humanities program, an arts council’s documentary program, or a state library’s local-history initiative. Start with the state government directory and follow links to administering agencies or partner organizations. Search both “grants” and “funding opportunities.”

Check whether a state program passes federal money through to local recipients; additional terms may apply. A program labeled “local” need not be administratively simple.

### Foundation

Search for funded work adjacent to yours. Read the acknowledgment page of a comparable archive, study, or exhibition. Look up the named foundation, then read its current priorities and recent awards.

Where available, a private foundation’s Form 990-PF can help you examine historical giving. The [IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/tax-exempt-organization-search) is one starting point for organization and filing information. Returns describe past activity and can lag. A past award to a similar project is a lead, not evidence of a current open call.

If a foundation says “invitation only,” record that fact rather than drafting an unsolicited proposal.

### Corporate

Search for employers, utilities, banks, and other companies operating in the place you serve. Read the community-giving rules before contacting a local office. A company may support only places where employees live or work, or route requests through an employee nomination process.

Distinguish a charitable grant from an event sponsorship that buys recognition or other benefits. If a sponsor requests publicity, check that the applicant can provide it without compromising participant permissions, research independence, or the library’s policies.

### Community

Find the community foundation serving the county and inspect its individual funds. One portal may contain a countywide grant, a town-specific endowment, and a fund restricted to youth activities.

Search city and county department pages for cultural, neighborhood, and accessibility programs. Ask a librarian or community foundation staff member a narrow question:

> Which local programs accept applications from individual historians, rather than only from incorporated charities?

A specific question is easier to route than “Who has money?”

### Run a repeatable search session

Spend one part of each session finding names and another part verifying them. For Mara, “oral history grant” is one search; “county cultural fund,” “public humanities fellowship,” and “community archive award” reach different listings. Log useful terms along with sources.

Stop when you have a few opportunities you can actually assess. Thirty unverified bookmarks create more work than three notices with clear next decisions.

## 3. Triage eligibility in 15 minutes

Use a timer. This is an elimination pass, not legal review or a substitute for reading the entire notice before applying. Mark each issue **yes**, **no**, or **unknown**, and copy the relevant sentence or page reference. An unknown is not a tentative yes.

| Minutes | Test | Decision question |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | Applicant | Can the proposed legal applicant apply in this cycle? |
| 3–6 | Place and purpose | Are the location, people, activities, and intended result within scope? |
| 6–9 | Money | Is the request within range, and can the applicant meet match and cash requirements? |
| 9–12 | Clock and workload | Can the work start, finish, and be reported within the permitted period? |
| 12–15 | Submission | Can you obtain every mandatory attachment, approval, and registration on time? |

In the first pass, look for exclusions. “Researchers are encouraged to participate” does not mean researchers may be lead applicants. “Nonprofits” may mean a particular tax status, not every volunteer group. A university affiliation might be required at application, at award, or throughout the term. Identify which date matters.

For the money test, imagine paying the first invoice. If a grant reimburses approved expenses after you document payment, who pays the transcriber first? A project can balance on paper and still be impossible to operate. Ask about advances or payment timing if the notice is silent.

For match, distinguish cash from donated services and check what counts, how it must be valued, and what proof is needed. Do not promise volunteer hours before confirming both the volunteers and the rule.

Finish with a disposition:

- **Pursue:** No known disqualifier; the project fits the program’s purpose, and the application effort is proportionate. Assign a drafting date.
- **Clarify:** One material fact could change the decision. Assign a person and a date to resolve it.
- **Pass:** A hard rule fails, the terms are unworkable, or the project would have to become something you do not want to deliver. Record the reason.
- **Watch:** A plausible future fit, but this cycle is closed or too early. Record a specific date to check again.

For example, suppose a county notice accepts libraries, prohibits individual applicants, and requires a collection to be publicly accessible within six months. Mara cannot apply directly. A library application is worth clarifying only if the library wants to lead and the access requirement permits some accounts to remain restricted.

Her email could read:

> The library would publish only interviews cleared by the speaker. Would a collection containing released interviews, with other recordings withheld, meet the access requirement on page three?

Save the answer. If no answer arrives, do not build the application on the favorable interpretation.

## 4. Write the one-page concept note

A concept note lets a reviewer or partner answer four questions without opening an attachment:

- Why this work?
- Why this team?
- What will exist afterward?
- What is the money buying?

Use the funder’s required format if there is one. Otherwise, write a short page with a descriptive title, a request, a project account, and a test of completion.

Start with a bounded gap, not a sweeping social diagnosis. Name the evidence that makes the gap visible. Then describe the work in sequence and give each important task an owner. Separate what you control from what you hope for. You can schedule interviews and offer speakers a chance to review them; you cannot promise what they will say or that they will permit release.

Here is a worked note. It assumes that the library has agreed to apply and that the grant permits the stated costs.

**Mill Creek bus route: a six-month oral-history collection**  
**Applicant:** Mill Creek Library | **Request:** $7,000

> Mill Creek Library requests $7,000 to document how residents used the former mill bus route. The local-history librarian’s inventory identifies route maps and timetables but no recorded rider accounts in the library’s collection. The maps show where the bus stopped; they do not tell a reader how people used those stops to reach a shift, a shop, or a relative’s home. We propose a small collection of first-person accounts, not a representative survey of former riders.
>
> Independent historian Mara will work with the librarian to invite participants through a public notice and local groups. In months one and two they will test the interview guide, explain recording and release options, and schedule interviews. Mara will aim to record eight interviews, each about an hour long. A participant payment recognizes the time spent in the interview and does not depend on permission to publish.
>
> In months three and four, a transcription provider will prepare draft transcripts. Mara will check them against the recordings and offer speakers a review period. The librarian will record each release decision and prepare catalog descriptions. In the final two months the library will post cleared materials in its local-history collection and hold a listening session using only excerpts approved for that purpose.
>
> We will report interviews completed, transcripts reviewed, accounts released, and listening-session attendance as separate figures. We will ask session visitors whether they could locate an account relevant to a question they brought. We will summarize responses without treating attendees as a sample of all residents. If fewer accounts are released than expected, the report will explain the difference without identifying speakers who declined.
>
> The request covers historian time, transcription, participant payments, catalog preparation, accessible event materials, and an access check. The library will provide the meeting space and maintain released files under its collection policy. The librarian will oversee permissions and financial reporting.

Before sending a note like this, substantiate the inventory, confirm the librarian’s duties, and verify that the collection policy covers the proposed files. If a fact is still being checked in an internal draft, label it. Do not promote a planned conversation to a “partnership.”

To adapt the note, compare it with the published criteria. If a funder values public access, show how someone will find the collection. If it values method, explain recruitment, limitations, and review.

Do not retrofit the project by claiming the listening session will improve transportation policy unless there is a credible path to that outcome. Reviewers can assess a modest result more readily than an untestable promise.

## 5. Build a budget a reviewer can reconstruct

Build from tasks, not from the grant ceiling. For each task, list the person or supplier, quantity, unit, rate, and basis for the estimate. Keep quotes or dated notes in a working folder. Enter the same numbers in the budget, narrative, and portal. Reconcile the totals again after any edit.

| Example cost | Calculation | Request |
|---|---:|---:|
| Historian: planning, interviews, review, coordination | 80 hours × $45 | $3,600 |
| Transcription | 480 recorded minutes × $2 | $960 |
| Participant payments | 8 interviews × $75 | $600 |
| Catalog preparation | 20 hours × $40 | $800 |
| Accessible event materials | 1 quoted package × $440 | $440 |
| Access check and corrections | 12 hours × $50 | $600 |
| **Total requested** | | **$7,000** |

These rates illustrate arithmetic, not a market survey. In a real budget, specify what the quoted package contains. Identify who does the catalog preparation and how this paid task differs from the librarian’s other duties.

If employees perform the work, use the applicant’s appropriate payroll and fringe treatment. If contracting, follow applicable procurement rules and the actual working relationship. Calling someone a contractor in a spreadsheet does not determine their legal status.

Draft a short justification for any line a reader might question. For instance:

> The transcription estimate assumes eight interviews averaging 60 minutes each. The unit is a recorded minute, not a minute of provider labor. The historian’s separate time includes checking names and place references against the audio.

This explains why transcription and transcript review are not duplicate charges.

Show the difference between **total project cost** and **request** when they differ. If another award pays for part of the project, identify the part and whether the funds are confirmed or pending. Do not charge two grants for the same hour. If the room is provided without charge and the funder does not require in-kind valuation, it may be enough to describe the space as available. Do not invent a rental invoice to enlarge the budget.

Check indirect costs, administration fees, taxes, travel, participant costs, and equipment against program rules. Do not assume either that overhead is forbidden or that a chosen percentage is allowed. If a fiscal sponsor charges a fee, calculate it on the base specified in the sponsor agreement and test whether the grant will cover it. If a required cost cannot be requested, identify a real funding source or reduce scope before applying.

Finally, make a cash calendar. For each month, enter:

- Opening cash available for the project
- Expected receipts, using conservative payment dates
- Payments due
- Closing cash, carried forward to the next month

Distinguish confirmed receipts from hoped-for ones. If $2,500 must be paid before the first reimbursement and only $900 is available, the gap is $1,600, even if the grant eventually covers every dollar. Resolve that gap with the applicant’s financial decision-maker. A hopeful payment date is not an advance.

## 6. Keep a tracking sheet that leads to action

Use one row per opportunity per cycle, not one row per funder. A program can change its priorities or deadline next year. A tiny team can use a shared spreadsheet; a solo researcher can use a local file with a backup. The minimum useful row tells you what the opportunity is, why it might work, and exactly what to do next.

Copy these headers into a sheet. The sample row is fictional.

| ID / cycle | Program / source | Applicant | Due / zone | Request | Gate / evidence | Status | Owner | Next action / date |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| MC-01 / spring | County Culture Fund / saved official notice | Library | Apr 18, 5 p.m. local | $7,000 | Partial release unclear; notice p. 3 | Clarify | Mara | Ask program officer by Mar 12 |

Add these columns as the opportunity advances:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Source checked | Date accessed, notice version, amendment link |
| Timing | Internal deadline, earliest start, end date, expected decision window |
| Dependencies | Registrations, letters, sponsor review, board or financial approval |
| Budget terms | Match, advance or reimbursement, cost restrictions |
| Submission record | Final folder link, timestamp, confirmation number |
| Decision | Awarded, declined, withdrawn, or no decision yet; date and amount |
| Post-decision | Next permitted inquiry, feedback, agreement review, report dates |
| Closeout | Final payment checked, reports accepted if confirmed, records retention date or rule |

Use status words consistently: **Found → Checking → Clarify/Pursue/Pass**, then, for live applications, **Drafting → Submitted → Awarded/Declined**. Add **Watch** for future cycles, **Withdrawn** where relevant, and **Closed** for completed awards.

“Submitted” requires a receipt or other evidence of delivery. “Awarded” does not mean money has been received; keep payment status separate.

At a weekly review, filter for overdue actions and deadlines in the next month. Every active row gets one owner and a dated next action. “Work on proposal” is too vague; “librarian approves release paragraph by Thursday” is usable.

Move passes out of the active view without deleting them. The note “requires university lead” can save you from checking the same unsuitable program twice.

Keep sensitive records elsewhere. The prospect sheet does not need participant details, bank information, portal passwords, or confidential reviewer correspondence. Link to appropriately restricted files rather than pasting them into a broadly shared tracker.

## 7. Assemble, check, and submit

Turn the instructions into a compliance list before drafting the full application. Use one line per required item:

- Exact prompt
- Word or character limit
- File type
- Responsible person
- Due date
- Completion check

Include items that are not essays: a board roster, determination letter, résumé, authorized signature, budget form, work sample, or partner letter. Mark something “not applicable” only where the instructions allow it.

Ask partners for evidence of a specific commitment. “We support this important project” tells a reviewer little about delivery. A useful library letter identifies the room, collection staff time, hosting responsibility, and any conditions still to be met. Send partners a factual outline and enough time to correct it; never submit an endorsement they have not approved.

Have one reader inspect the application as if they knew nothing about the project. Ask them to locate:

- The request
- The applicant
- The project period
- The number of interviews
- The consent process
- The evidence of completion

Have another reader, or yourself on a separate pass, inspect mechanics: names match legal records, attachments open, links work without private credentials, budget totals agree, and the final page has not been cut off in conversion.

Set an internal deadline earlier than the official one. Enter long answers in the portal early enough to discover character limits or formatting problems. Preview or download the submission if the system permits.

Save the final submitted files, instructions, receipt, and any help-desk correspondence in a dated folder. If the portal fails, follow the notice’s technical-support process promptly. A screenshot can document what happened; it does not entitle you to a late submission.

## 8. Follow up without turning follow-up into a campaign

Immediately after submission, verify delivery and record the decision window. If there is no confirmation, inspect the portal status and contact the listed support channel. Distinguish “draft saved,” “submitted,” and “accepted for review” when the system does. Do not send a second copy to unrelated staff unless instructed.

If the announced window passes and inquiries are allowed, send one concise status question:

> **Subject:** Status inquiry — Mill Creek Library, application MC-01  
>
> We submitted *Mill Creek bus route* on April 16 and received confirmation 24-118. The notice indicated decisions were expected in June. Could you let us know whether there is a revised notification window? Thank you.

Do not attach a revised proposal, announce new partners, or lobby reviewers unless the program invites updates. If a significant fact changes before decision, such as loss of the applicant organization, check the instructions for required notification rather than waiting for an award.

For a decline, read the message before replying. Some programs provide scores; others prohibit individual feedback. Where permitted, ask a question the staff can answer:

> Was the main concern the evidence of demand, the collection method, or fit with this cycle’s priorities?

Record what they actually say. Separate reviewer comments from your own hypotheses. A decline does not by itself show that the project was weak, and a resubmission is not automatically worthwhile.

## 9. Manage the award from agreement to final report

Before accepting, compare the offer with the application. Check:

- Award amount and period
- Allowable expenses
- Payment schedule
- Publicity requirements
- Data or intellectual-property terms
- Reporting requirements
- Procedures for changes

If the grantmaker offers $4,500 against a $7,000 plan, do not quietly promise the original scope. Propose a smaller complete project and get the revised budget and deliverables approved in writing before commitment.

Build a one-page award calendar. Put each deliverable beside an owner, evidence source, internal review date, and funder due date. Set reminders before, not just on, the due date.

If you are an individual, schedule the time to reconcile receipts and write the report as part of delivery. If you are a small organization, identify a second person who can locate the agreement and reporting instructions if the lead becomes unavailable.

Use a ledger with:

- Date
- Payee
- Purpose
- Budget line
- Amount
- Payment status
- Supporting document reference

Reconcile it to financial records at regular intervals and before each payment request.

Maintain a small evidence log alongside it: interviews completed, releases received, catalog entries created, and event attendance counted by a stated method. Collect only the personal information needed, use suitable safeguards, and report aggregates where appropriate. A grant report does not override a participant’s privacy choice.

When the plan changes, check the agreement before moving money or extending work. Some changes require advance written approval; others may be within the recipient’s discretion. Ask with specifics:

> Two interviews ran shorter than expected. May we shift up to $160 in unused transcription funds to additional access testing, without changing the completion date?

File the answer with the budget version it authorizes. Silence is not approval.

A final narrative can be short and accountable:

1. **Commitment:** Restate the approved objective and period.
2. **Delivery:** Compare planned and actual work using the same units.
3. **Evidence:** Identify records or permitted public links that support the account.
4. **Variance:** Explain what differed, why, and any approved change.
5. **Finance:** Reconcile spending to the approved budget and explain the balance.
6. **Stewardship:** State who maintains the output and what remains uncertain.

For example:

> We planned eight interviews and completed seven. Six speakers approved public release. All six cleared transcripts are cataloged; the seventh interview is not part of the public collection. Fourteen people attended the listening session, and nine returned optional feedback cards. Seven of those nine reported finding an account relevant to their question.

This states what was observed without assigning the nine respondents’ views to all visitors.

After filing, save the report and delivery confirmation. Check whether the grantmaker requires acknowledgment of acceptance, return of unspent funds, a final invoice, or retention of particular records. Follow the applicable agreement and recordkeeping obligations rather than picking a universal retention period. Close the tracker row only after financial and reporting tasks are settled.

## 10. Ten rejection reasons and their fixes

These are diagnostic possibilities, not conclusions to draw from a form letter. Where feedback exists, use it. Where it does not, inspect the proposal against the published criteria before deciding what to change.

| Reason | What it can look like | Fix before the next submission |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Ineligible applicant | An unaffiliated researcher applies to an institution-only program. | Find an individual award or confirm a genuinely eligible lead with a defined role. Do not resubmit under a borrowed identity. |
| 2. Wrong fit | Local archival work is pitched to a program that funds policy implementation. | Compare the project’s actual result with the stated purpose. Choose another program rather than renaming the same output. |
| 3. Administrative failure | A required letter is missing, the file is unreadable, or the deadline has passed. | Build an attachment list from the current instructions and check the submitted package, not just the working folder. |
| 4. Unsupported need | The proposal says “no one has documented this” without checking existing collections. | Bound the claim. Cite a dated inventory, a search method, or a documented partner observation; acknowledge what you have not checked. |
| 5. Unclear method | The reader cannot see how participants are recruited or how evidence becomes an output. | Describe the sequence, owner, decision points, permissions, and limits of the method. |
| 6. Implausible scope | One part-time researcher promises a large collection, exhibition, and evaluation in a short award period. | Estimate hours by task, include review time, and remove an entire deliverable if necessary. |
| 7. Unconvincing budget | Round sums have no cost basis, or the narrative promises work absent from the budget. | Rebuild the budget from units and rates; map each material task to a funded or confirmed contributed resource. |
| 8. Weak delivery evidence | A partner is named but has not agreed to host, recruit, or preserve anything. | Secure a precise commitment, show relevant prior work, or assign the task to someone with demonstrated capacity. |
| 9. Outcome overclaim | Attendance is presented as proof of lasting behavior change. | Report attendance as attendance. Select a feasible measure for any further claim and disclose its limits. |
| 10. Competitive selection | An eligible, credible proposal is not selected within the available funds or portfolio. | Seek permitted feedback, retain the sound components, and assess another suitable cycle or grantmaker without inventing a flaw. |

Avoid the reflex to rewrite every paragraph after a rejection. If the problem was a missing signature, the corrective tool is a submission check, not a new theory of change. If the project requires a confidentiality promise the grant terms do not allow, better prose will not resolve the conflict. The right fix may be to pass.

## 11. Put the system to work this week

In the first work session, finish the five-sentence brief and name the legal applicant.

In the second, find one official lead from each relevant source category; skip categories that plainly do not fit.

In the third, run the timed gate on the strongest leads and send only the clarification questions that could change a decision. Enter next actions before closing the sheet.

For one opportunity that passes, draft the concept note and build the unit budget side by side. Read them against each other:

- If the note promises accessible transcripts, the budget or a confirmed contribution must pay for the work.
- If the budget buys a service that never appears in the method, explain it or remove it.
- If the project requires cash before the first grant payment, check the first month’s cash requirement before asking anyone for a signature.

The deliverable at the end of the week need not be an application. It can be a documented pass, a verified invitation to a future cycle, or a partner decision that makes the next search more precise.

None guarantees funding. Each leaves you with something more useful than a bookmark: a reasoned decision, an accountable owner, and a next step you can afford to take.
